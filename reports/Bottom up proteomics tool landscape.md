# Build validators, not engines, for proteomics

The best opening for a single developer in bottom-up proteomics in late 2026 is **validation and diagnostics tooling that reads existing tools' outputs**. Building another search engine, format converter or statistics port is the wrong move. The strongest documented gap is FDR trustworthiness: a 2025 *Nature Methods* entrapment study found that **no DIA tool tested (DIA-NN, Spectronaut, EncyclopeDIA) consistently controls FDR**, and single-cell data was the worst case. The reference implementation of the fix, FDRBench, is Java with a GUI and is not on PyPI ([Wen et al., PMC12240826](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12240826/); [FDRBench](https://github.com/Noble-Lab/FDRBench)). Close behind are an auditor that checks SDRF metadata against the actual data, a Python cohort-QC and drift tool that writes mzQC, and Python PTM-site statistics. The rest of the landscape fills fast. On 2026-10-13, the day of this research, bigbio released `qpx`, a universal converter to Parquet. omicverse ported MSstats, proDA and DEqMS to Python on a single day in May 2026. An AI-assisted SDRF corpus already covers 27.8% of PRIDE ([qpx](https://pypi.org/project/qpx/); [pymsstats](https://pypi.org/project/pymsstats/); [sdrf-annotated-datasets](https://github.com/bigbio/sdrf-annotated-datasets)). The practical rule is to sit on top of the open interop layers (psm_utils, qpx, pymzqc, sdrf-pipelines) and not compete with them. The first audience should be bioinformaticians and core facilities, served through a library, a CLI and a static HTML report. Wet-lab GUIs are a crowded field of single-format Shiny apps.

## The upstream stack is mature, and its gaps are licensing and FDR

**Raw data access is largely solved for Thermo and Bruker.** ThermoRawFileParser 2.0-dev moves to .NET 8 without Mono and builds self-contained binaries for the major platforms. It writes mzML, MGF and its own Parquet dialect ([TRFP releases](https://github.com/compomics/ThermoRawFileParser/releases)). DIA-NN 2.1 reads .raw natively on Linux ([DIA-NN releases](https://github.com/vdemichev/DiaNN/releases)). On the Bruker side, the Rust crate `timsrust` (v0.6.7, Oct 2026, now under MannLabs) and the Python packages `alphatims` and `alpharaw` cover timsTOF ([crates.io timsrust](https://crates.io/api/v1/crates/timsrust); [PyPI alpharaw](https://pypi.org/pypi/alpharaw/json)). One small interop gap remains: the Python binding `timsrust_pyo3` is stuck at 0.4.1 from Dec 2024 ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/timsrust_pyo3/json)). The format layer is in transition. **mzPeak**, a Parquet-based successor to mzML, was published in *JPR* in October 2025 and is chartered by HUPO-PSI, but I found no evidence that it has been ratified ([PMC12604042](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12604042/); [PSI charter](https://www.psidev.info/mass-spectrometry-standards-working-group-charter)). Meanwhile TRFP, Sage and DIA-NN each write their own Parquet schema. Reader libraries are healthy in Rust (`mzdata` 0.67.4) and Python (pyteomics 5.0.1, pyopenms 3.6.0, pymzml 2.7.0) ([crates.io mzdata](https://crates.io/api/v1/crates/mzdata); [PyPI pyopenms](https://pypi.org/pypi/pyopenms/json)).

**Search and DIA are dominated by tools that open pipelines cannot redistribute.** MSFragger/FragPipe is free only for academic, non-commercial use, and commercial licensing has gone through Fragmatics LLC since 1 May 2025 ([U-M licensing](https://available-inventions.umich.edu/product/msfragger-ultrafast-and-comprehensive-identification-of-peptides-from-tandem-mass-spectra)). DIA-NN 2.x ships binary-only "for academic use", with industry distribution through Aptila Biotech ([DIA-NN releases](https://github.com/vdemichev/DiaNN/releases)). Spectronaut, Proteome Discoverer, PEAKS and Mascot are commercial. The open alternatives are **Sage** (Rust, MIT, fast) and **AlphaDIA** (Apache-2.0; *Nature Biotechnology* 2025; v2.2.0 released 2026-10-08). Sage's stable line has stalled at v0.14.7, and its diaPASEF and ion-mobility LFQ support is still in a v0.15 beta marked "not ready for production-use" ([Sage releases](https://github.com/lazear/sage/releases); [PyPI alphadia](https://pypi.org/pypi/alphadia/json)). Even quantms, the flagship open nf-core pipeline, delegates DIA to the closed DIA-NN ([quantms, Nat Methods 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41592-024-02343-1)). A 2025 *JPR* perspective says this reliance on proprietary software limits reproducibility and imposes hidden costs ([Perez-Riverol et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12053954/)). An open, permissively licensed DIA engine is a real gap, but it is not a few-weeks project.

**Rescoring and prediction are crowded and well maintained.** The current releases are MS2Rescore 4.0.2, Oktoberfest 0.11.1, MS2PIP 4.2.0, DeepLC 4.5.0, AlphaPeptDeep 1.5.1 and the Koina model-serving client ([PyPI ms2rescore](https://pypi.org/pypi/ms2rescore/json); [PyPI deeplc](https://pypi.org/pypi/deeplc/json); [PyPI koinapy](https://pypi.org/pypi/koinapy/json)). De novo sequencing is an active ML area: Casanovo 5.2.1 was released in Aug 2026 and InstaNovo 1.2.2 in Dec 2025. NovoBench exists because evaluation datasets have "no consensus" ([PyPI casanovo](https://pypi.org/pypi/casanovo/json); [NovoBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.11906)). Two thinner spots stand out. mokapot, the Python Percolator, has not had a PyPI release since Sept 2023, and pyAscore (PTM localization) has not had one since Dec 2023 ([PyPI mokapot](https://pypi.org/pypi/mokapot/json); [PyPI pyAscore](https://pypi.org/pypi/pyAscore/json)). PSM-level interop is handled by **psm_utils**, which reads 22 formats, including DIA-NN and Sage Parquet, MaxQuant msms.txt and PD MSF ([psm_utils](https://github.com/compomics/psm_utils)).

## Downstream tooling has consolidated faster than the literature suggests

Several apparent gaps closed during 2026, and this is the most important correction to conventional wisdom. **Converting outputs into one tidy schema is no longer open.** `qpx` 1.1.6 converts OpenMS, DIA-NN, MaxQuant, Spectronaut, FragPipe, CPTAC CDAP, mzIdentML and SDRF to validated Parquet. It also adds DirectLFQ, MaxLFQ, iBAQ and TopN quantification, SQL querying and MuData export ([PyPI qpx](https://pypi.org/project/qpx/)). This supersedes the stalled `quantmsio` 0.0.3 ([PyPI quantmsio](https://pypi.org/pypi/quantmsio/json)). MSstatsConvert already covers about 17 upstream tools in R ([NAMESPACE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Vitek-Lab/MSstatsConvert/devel/NAMESPACE)). **Python statistics ports exist now.** `pymsstats` 0.3.0 claims full coverage of the MSstats 4.14.2 API, with bit-for-bit summarization (self-reported). `pyproda`, `pydeqms` and `pylimma` arrived in 2026, and CompOmics has an early MSqRobPy that does not fit mixed models ([pymsstats](https://pypi.org/project/pymsstats/); [pylimma](https://pypi.org/project/pylimma/); [MSqRobPy](https://github.com/CompOmics/MSqRobPy)). Single-cell proteomics in the AnnData/scverse style is covered by scpviz (*JOSS* 2026) ([JOSS](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.10303)).

Protein roll-up is commoditized. The options are MaxLFQ, iq's C++ `fast_MaxLFQ`, directLFQ, FlashLFQ, AlphaQuant and TMT-Integrator, all actively pushed in 2026 ([directLFQ](https://github.com/MannLabs/directlfq); [iq](https://github.com/tvpham/iq); [FlashLFQ](https://github.com/smith-chem-wisc/FlashLFQ)). The statistics layer is still mostly R and Bioconductor: MSstats, msqrob2/QFeatures, prolfqua, proDA and protti are all active, while DEP and Proteus have been idle since 2022–2023. Wet-lab web apps keep multiplying without consolidating. FragPipe-Analyst reads only FragPipe output, LFQ-Analyst reads MaxQuant `proteinGroups.txt`, and ProVision, QProMS, TraianProt, ProtVis and Dexdash each re-implement the volcano-plot loop ([FragPipe-Analyst docs](https://fragpipe-analyst-doc.nesvilab.org/Formatting.html); [TraianProt](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.15806)). For QC, **mzQC 1.0.0** became final in Feb 2024, with Python, R and Java libraries ([psidev mzQC](https://www.psidev.info/mzqc); [JASMS 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11311537/)). `pmultiqc` produces interactive HTML QC for quantms, MaxQuant, DIA-NN, FragPipe, mhcquant and QPX, but its description does not mention mzQC output ([PyPI pmultiqc](https://pypi.org/project/pmultiqc/)). Longitudinal statistical process control lives in R (MSstatsQC) or in older services such as QCloud (2017) ([MSstatsQC](https://bioc.r-universe.dev/articles/MSstatsQC/MSstatsQC.html); [QCloud](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5764250/)). Spectrum visualization is also not a gap: PDV, spectrum_utils, pyopenms-viz and the USI viewers at PRIDE, ProteomeCentral and Quetzal cover it ([ProteomeXchange 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12807779/)).

Public data is abundant and under-reused, which makes good test material easy to find. ProteomeXchange held **64,330 datasets** through June 2025, 77% of them in PRIDE, with a record 10,686 submissions in 2024 ([PMC12807779](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12807779/)). Under 10% of public PRIDE datasets are mentioned as reanalysed ([PRIDE 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701690/)). Programmatic access is available through the PRIDE REST v2/v3 APIs, `pridepy`, `ppx`, PROXI and USIs. SDRF metadata is the main reuse bottleneck. Only **1.6%** of 9,671 PRIDE submissions from mid-2022 onward carried submitter SDRF ([lesSDRF, Nat Commun 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-42543-5)). bigbio's AI-assisted corpus now holds 11,907 accessions, but sample fields are sparse: disease is filled in 43% of records and age in 9% ([sdrf-annotated-datasets](https://github.com/bigbio/sdrf-annotated-datasets)).

## Users keep hitting FDR doubt, format drift, memory walls and metadata holes

The best-evidenced pain point is **FDR validity**. Wen et al. showed that of three common entrapment-based ways to check FDR, one is invalid, one gives only a lower bound, and one is valid but under-powered. They proposed a more powerful estimator and found that DIA tools fail to control FDR consistently ([Nat Methods 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-025-02719-x)). ProteoBench turned this into a hosted "DIA Ion Entrapment – Astral" module. Its preprint warns that performance claims for new workflows are "often incomplete and biased" toward the authors' own tool ([ProteoBench module](https://proteobench.readthedocs.io/en/latest/available-modules/active-modules/13-entrapment-ion-dia-astral/); [ProteoBench preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2025.12.09.692895.full.pdf)). Workflow variance is large. One differential-abundance benchmark enumerated **1,428 DIA workflows** and found that outcomes swung with library, normalization and test choices ([Fröhlich et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9098472)). Specialist domains repeat the FDR problem with their own twists. In glycoproteomics, "the meaning of FDR depends on the assumptions behind each algorithm" ([PMC8398183](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8398183/)). Immunopeptidomics loses identifications to non-tryptic search-space inflation, which ML rescoring partly recovers ([Proteomics review](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/chemrxiv/article-details/64f75229dd1a73847f3f28b6)). Metaproteomics lacks a standard FDR approach for huge databases ([PMC9925844](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9925844/)).

Issue trackers repeat three themes, though as recurring examples rather than measured prevalence. The first is **format drift breaking converters**. DIA-NN issue #1088, a `DIANNtoMSstatsFormat` failure on a renamed column, drew 21 comments ([DiaNN #1088](https://github.com/vdemichev/DiaNN/issues/1088)). Downstream readers such as proteopy now branch on the DIA-NN version string ([proteopy](https://proteopy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api/generated/proteopy.read.diann.html)). The second is **memory failures**: 41 FragPipe issues match out-of-memory searches, including a 6.4 GB metaproteomics FASTA that fails even with an 800 GB Java heap ([FragPipe #2843](https://github.com/Nesvilab/FragPipe/issues/2843)). The third is **confusion over normalization and column meanings** in downstream analysis ([DiaNN #1929](https://github.com/vdemichev/DiaNN/issues/1929); [#1772](https://github.com/vdemichev/DiaNN/issues/1772)). PTM work has a specific reporting gap. The authors of `sitereport` state that "Spectronaut and DIA-NN do not offer a common reporting scheme for phosphosites" ([PMC11239223](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11239223/)). A 2026 PNNL paper on peptide-to-site roll-up strategies shows the methods question is still open ([PNNL](https://www.pnnl.gov/publications/aggregation-methods-quantifying-ptm-and-structural-changes-bottom-proteomics)). The community's own agenda points the same way. EuBIC-MS 2025 chose its hackathons on single-cell analysis, FAIR metadata extraction, R–Python interoperability and DIA validation ([EuBIC 2025](https://bia.unibz.it/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Community-driven-advances-in-computational-mass-spectrometry/991007399553301241)). A Human Protein Atlas reanalysis project names file conversion, metadata gaps and workflow restarts as its bottlenecks ([NAISS 7699](https://nim.nsc.liu.se/projects/7699)).

**Audience choice.** The evidence favours bioinformaticians, tool developers and core facilities as the first users. Their pain points (FDR validation, drift detection, metadata auditing) are concrete. The wet-lab tier is saturated with single-format Shiny apps, and supporting many vendor and version inputs for non-programmers is exactly the maintenance burden that breaks existing tools. A static HTML report on top of a library can still reach wet-lab users later. No 2023–2026 survey quantifies wet-lab demand for GUIs or Windows support, so this recommendation is inference.

## Six buildable projects, ranked

The table ranks each idea on usefulness, feasibility in 2–4 weeks on CPU only, and novelty against tools that exist today. All six should read inputs through psm_utils or qpx rather than writing their own parsers. That choice saves weeks and keeps the tool complementary to the bigbio and CompOmics ecosystems.

| Rank | Project | Usefulness | Feasibility | Novelty | Main threat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `entrap`: Python entrapment-FDR validation | High | High | Medium-high | Noble lab ships a Python port |
| 2 | `sdrf-audit`: SDRF-vs-data consistency checker | High | High | High | bigbio CI adds semantic checks |
| 3 | `qcdrift`: cohort QC/drift detection, writes mzQC | Medium-high | High | Medium | pmultiqc adds mzQC and longitudinal views |
| 4 | `sitequant`: Python PTM-site roll-up and protein-adjusted tests | Medium-high | Medium | Medium | omicverse ports MSstatsPTM; AlphaQuant overlap unverified |
| 5 | `normbench`: normalization × imputation benchmark harness | Medium | High | Low-medium | PRONE and NAguideR already cover the concept |
| 6 | Immunopeptidomics QC report | Medium (niche) | High | Medium | Small community; pmultiqc covers mhcquant |

### 1. Entrapment FDR validation as a Python library

**What it is.** A pip-installable library and CLI with three parts. It builds entrapment FASTAs (shuffled or foreign-species, at a chosen fold *r*, with peptide-pair files). It implements the lower-bound, combined and paired FDP estimators of Wen et al. at precursor, peptide and protein-group level. It reads DIA-NN, FragPipe, Sage, Spectronaut and MaxQuant outputs through psm_utils. It outputs FDP-vs-FDR curves with bootstrap confidence bands and JSON/mzQC-style metrics.

**Closest tools and how it differs.** FDRBench is the Java CLI and GUI reference implementation, with an R plotting helper ([FDRBench](https://github.com/Noble-Lab/FDRBench)). ProteoBench's entrapment module is a hosted benchmark on one fixed Astral dataset. This project differs by being Python-native and importable from notebooks, ProteoBench or quantms. It adds protein-group handling, uncertainty bands, and batch evaluation across runs and parameter sets. No `entrapment`, `fdrbench` or `pyentrapment` package existed on PyPI as of 2026-10-13.

**Test data.** Search PXD001819 (yeast-UPS1 DDA) with Sage against a 1:1 shuffled entrapment database ([PRIDE PXD001819](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pride/archive/projects/PXD001819)). For DIA, use PXD028735 (human/yeast/E. coli LFQ benchmark) ([PRIDE PXD028735](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pride/archive/projects/PXD028735)).

**MVP (2–3 weeks).** The FASTA generator, the three estimators validated number-for-number against FDRBench on identical input, readers for DIA-NN Parquet, Sage and FragPipe, and one worked example. The tool comes with a built-in publishable finding, the measured FDP of open tools on public data, which helps adoption.

**Risks.** Novelty rests on integration, not on the maths. DIA validation needs DIA-NN, which is academic-only, or AlphaDIA, whose CPU runtime is unverified. I did not confirm whether the authors' own entrapment search results are deposited.

### 2. Auditing AI-written SDRF against the actual data

**What it is.** A checker that compares an SDRF, increasingly LLM-generated, against what the dataset actually shows. Instrument model and dissociation type are read from streamed mzML headers. Run file names are reconciled against PRIDE file lists via pridepy or ppx. Declared modifications are compared with modification frequencies observed in deposited PSMs. TMT channels are checked against reporter-ion presence, and the declared organism against the taxonomy of identified proteins. The output is a scored discrepancy report that can fill SDRF v1.1.0's new "validation status" column ([SDRF spec](https://github.com/bigbio/proteomics-sample-metadata/blob/master/sdrf-proteomics/README.adoc)).

**Closest tools and how it differs.** sdrf-pipelines validates syntax and ontology terms only ([sdrf-pipelines](https://github.com/bigbio/sdrf-pipelines)). lesSDRF guides annotation, and sdrfcheck dates from 2020. None of them checks annotations against spectra or identifications (an inference from their documentation). Need is rising: every one of the 11,907 corpus accessions is AI-assisted, and a merged PR records a validator missing semantic errors in acquisition-method terms ([PR #641](https://github.com/bigbio/sdrf-annotated-datasets/pull/641)).

**Test data.** Use the curated "gold standard" SDRFs in sdrf-annotated-datasets, with deliberately corrupted copies as negative controls.

**MVP (about 2 weeks).** File-list reconciliation, the instrument check, the organism and modification check where search results are deposited, an HTML/JSON report, and a run over about 200 corpus SDRFs to estimate an error rate. That estimate is itself publishable.

**Risks.** Many projects deposit only vendor RAW files. Deep checks on those need ThermoRawFileParser and full downloads, so the MVP should be limited to projects with mzML or deposited results. bigbio could fold similar checks into its CI.

### 3. Cohort drift detection that speaks mzQC

**What it is.** A tool that ingests hundreds to thousands of runs from DIA-NN, FragPipe, MaxQuant or QPX. For each run it computes about 15 metrics: ID counts, intensity distributions, RT and mass-error drift, missingness and standard-peptide behaviour. It then applies EWMA/CUSUM control charts and change-point detection, plus PCA against SDRF batch factors, and writes mzQC files and a static dashboard.

**Closest tools and how it differs.** MSstatsQC (R) does longitudinal process control. pmultiqc (Python) produces per-project HTML without advertised mzQC output. pymzqc only reads and writes the format. proBatch (R) handles batch diagnostics ([proBatch tutorial](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8447595/)). This project would be the Python-native, multi-tool, cohort-scale tool that emits the standard, and it could be framed as a pmultiqc companion rather than a rival.

**Test data.** Any multi-batch PRIDE dataset with deposited search results, using batch labels from curated SDRFs. I did not pin a specific accession.

**MVP (2–3 weeks).** Three readers, the metric set with pymzqc export, control-chart flags and one HTML report. This project is the most wet-lab-adjacent of the six, because core-facility staff could read the report directly.

**Risks.** Moderate novelty, since the value is integration. Custom metrics need PSI-MS CV terms. bigbio could add the same feature.

### 4. Python PTM-site statistics across DIA-NN and FragPipe

**What it is.** A tool that maps peptidoforms to protein-residue sites using the FASTA, filters by localization probability, and rolls up to site level while handling multiply modified peptides. It tests site changes adjusted for protein abundance (site log fold change minus protein log fold change, with combined variance, following MSstatsPTM) on top of pymsstats or pylimma. It exports sequence windows for kinase-enrichment tools.

**Closest tools and how it differs.** MSstatsPTM and msqrob2PTM implement this model but only in R ([MSstatsPTM manual](https://bioconductor.statistik.tu-dortmund.de/packages/3.16/bioc/manuals/MSstatsPTM/man/MSstatsPTM.pdf); [msqrob2PTM](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.07.05.547780.full.pdf)). ProteoMeter is Python but targets LiP-MS and structural integration, and its authors note a "scarcity" of Python proteomics pipelines ([ProteoMeter](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.14.664747.full.pdf)). `sitereport` addresses site reporting but not protein-adjusted testing. This project differs as a multi-tool Python mapper with protein-adjusted inference.

**Test data.** Validate against MSstatsPTM's bundled example data. I did not identify a public phosphoproteomics PXD with deposited DIA-NN or FragPipe outputs, and that has to be settled before starting.

**MVP (3–4 weeks).** Site mapping for DIA-NN and FragPipe, site roll-up, the adjusted contrast, and parity checks against MSstatsPTM.

**Risks.** Each tool defines localization scores differently. omicverse could port MSstatsPTM next, as it did MSstats. I did not check whether AlphaQuant already provides site-level differential analysis, so check that first.

### 5. Normalization and imputation benchmark harness

**What it is.** A plug-in registry of about 8 normalizers and 8 imputers. Each combination is scored on masking-based error (MCAR/MNAR) and on spike-in ground truth: fold-change accuracy and DE recall/FDR on HYE or UPS1 mixtures. Results go to a leaderboard.

**Closest tools and how it differs.** PRONE (R) evaluates normalization and supports only one mixed imputation method ([PMC12058466](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12058466/)). NAguideR (R) evaluates 23 imputation methods ([PMC7641313](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7641313/)). PIMMS compares mainly its own deep imputers ([pimms](https://github.com/RasmussenLab/pimms)). ProteoBench benchmarks whole pipelines, not individual steps. This project differs by being Python-native and scoring normalization, imputation and DE jointly. Its value is real because existing imputation evidence comes mostly from single datasets run by method authors. One single-cell DIA benchmark found clustering more sensitive to normalization than to imputation ([Nat Commun 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65174-4)).

**Test data.** PXD028735 and PXD001819, using deposited outputs or re-searching them.

**Risks.** The concept is crowded, and PRONE's authors find normalization effects "highly dataset-specific", so a leaderboard can mislead if it is read as universal.

### 6. Immunopeptidomics QC report

**What it is.** From a FragPipe, Sage, mhcquant or MaxQuant result plus HLA alleles from SDRF, the report shows length distributions, the predicted-binder fraction per allele (MHCflurry 2.3.14, CPU), motif deconvolution, and contaminant and non-specific cleavage rates ([PyPI mhcflurry](https://pypi.org/project/mhcflurry/)).

**Closest tools and how it differs.** IPTK has not been released since Aug 2022 ([PyPI IPTKL](https://pypi.org/project/IPTKL/)). pmultiqc already ingests mhcquant output. This project differs by adding binding-prediction and motif-based QC. The domain is a documented FDR weak spot, but the user community is small.

**Test data.** I did not identify a specific public dataset.

**MVP (about 2 weeks).**

**Risks.** The MHCflurry model download size is unverified, the community is small, and the overlap with pmultiqc's mhcquant mode could grow.

## Directions to avoid, and claims that remain unverified

Several directions are already well served and should be avoided:

- **A universal output converter.** Use `qpx` or psm_utils instead.
- **LLM-based SDRF annotation.** bigbio's AI-assisted corpus and the OmicsFM agentic pipeline already cover it ([OmicsFM](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.25.747021v1.full)).
- **Python ports of MSstats, proDA, DEqMS or limma.** These exist now.
- **A single-cell Python toolkit.** scpviz covers this.
- **Generic HTML QC or results reports.** pmultiqc and alphastats cover these.
- **Spectrum or USI viewers.** Several already exist.
- **Another protein roll-up algorithm.**
- **Another Shiny differential-expression app.**
- **A "PXD quick-run" reanalysis wrapper.** It duplicates quantms and quantmsdiann ([quantmsdiann preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10319687)).

New search engines, open DIA engines and glyco or metaproteomics search engines are real gaps. They need far more than a few weeks and deep domain validation. Contributing to Sage's stalled v0.15 or to AlphaDIA is a better route. A tested mzPeak reader and writer in Rust and Python is a plausible later bet, but the spec is not ratified and the existing implementations could not be verified.

Several claims in the source research could not be verified and should be checked before relying on them:

- **GitHub metrics.** GitHub API access was blocked for part of the research, so star counts and commit activity are incomplete. Several release years (Sage v0.14.7, FragPipe 24.0) were inferred from pages that showed no year.
- **Self-reported claims.** pymsstats's bit-for-bit MSstats parity is the authors' claim. directLFQ's speed and accuracy claims, and Lupine's imputation results, are the authors' own benchmarks.
- **Unfetched pages.** The mzPeak full text and the ProteoBench entrapment module page could not be fetched.
- **Untested assumptions.** I did not test whether the Wen et al. data are deposited, whether AlphaQuant covers PTM sites, AlphaDIA's CPU runtime, or DIA-NN's licence terms for container use.
- **Stale or uncertain figures.** The 1.6% SDRF figure dates from 2023, and no newer count was found. The "quantmsdiann" attribution of a 59% protein-group gain came from a search summary and is unconfirmed.
- **Missing community evidence.** There is no 2023–2026 survey of user needs, GUI preference or Windows dependency. Reddit, mailing lists and Bioconductor support were not searched.

## Conclusion

The field has moved from "missing infrastructure" to "missing trust". Readers, converters, statistics ports and pipelines now exist in open, active form, and the bigbio ecosystem absorbs new interop niches within months. What remains scarce is independent evidence that a given result is right: that the FDR holds, that the metadata matches the data, that a cohort has not drifted. These are also the questions closed engines cannot answer about themselves. A developer who builds a verifier on top of psm_utils or qpx gets two things a converter cannot offer: a defensible niche that ecosystem maintainers are unlikely to fill themselves, and a built-in research result, such as measured FDP on public data or an error rate in AI-curated SDRFs, that turns a few weeks of code into a citable contribution.

The recommended sequence is to start with `entrap` or `sdrf-audit` because their MVPs are smallest and their results most publishable. Release with a CLI, a library API and a static HTML report aimed at bioinformaticians and core facilities. Before writing code, contact the Noble lab and the bigbio maintainers, because a short conversation is the cheapest way to avoid being duplicated.
